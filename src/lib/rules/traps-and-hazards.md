# Traps and Hazards

## Traps

The Underways are full of things left behind by people who did not want to be followed. Some traps are ancient — builder mechanisms still wound tight after centuries. Others are newer: snares set by goblins, wards scratched into stone by desperate travelers, or spells that outlived the caster who laid them.

### Spotting Traps

A character who takes the time to examine a specific area or object will find any trap present. No roll needed — deliberate caution is rewarded. Only call for a check (INT or WIS) when the character is rushing, distracted, or the mechanism is genuinely unlike anything they've encountered.

### Dealing with Traps

A character who understands the mechanism can attempt to disarm it. Rogues and anyone with relevant experience (tinker, locksmith, sapper) can neutralize most traps by describing a plausible method. If the approach is sound and time isn't pressing, it works. When haste or complexity demands a roll, the GM sets a DC.

### Designing Good Traps

Every trap should have a **tell** — a scuff mark, a faint smell, a section of wall that's too clean. Players who engage with the environment should be rewarded with warnings. Traps that offer no clue and no counterplay produce frustration, not tension.

## Hazards

Hazards are persistent dangers woven into the environment — things to navigate around, endure, or avoid entirely. Unlike traps, they are generally visible to anyone paying attention. The question is not whether you notice the boiling spring, but whether you can find a way past it.

### Types of Hazard

- **Obstruction.** Blocks or slows movement. Characters must find alternate routes, spend time clearing a path, or push through at cost.
- **Harm.** Deals ongoing damage to anyone in the area. Exposure is the enemy — the longer you stay, the worse it gets.
- **Sapping.** Drains capability without dealing direct damage — suppressing magic, dulling senses, exhausting the body. Often the most insidious because the effects are not immediately obvious.
- **Layered.** The worst hazards combine categories. A passage choked with razor fungus (harm) growing over unstable scree (obstruction) is far more dangerous than either alone.
